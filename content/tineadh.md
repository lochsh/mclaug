Title: Exploring the LASID: labial fricative realisations of word-final broad «dh»
Slug: teineadh
Date: 10th October 2026

## A curious pronunciation

Sometime last year I was listening to a relation of mine on the [Doegen Records
web project](https://www.doegen.ie/LA_1262d1). He was from Tamhnach Mhullaigh
in Donegal, and was recorded in 1931. His pronunciation of the word
_teineadh_ ("fire", genitive singular) caught my attention:

> Shuigh sí a chos na teineadh agus bhí thoisigh sí ag caoineadh ar ais.

I make the pronunciation of _teineadh_ as something like <span
class=ipa>/t′enəv/</span>, or more obnoxiously <span class=ipa>[tɕe̞nɪ̠β]</span>.
It certainly ends with some kind of voiced
labial fricative[^bilabial], which is what I found so unexpected. I would have
expected the word to end in something like <span class=ipa>[u~ʉ(w)]</span>
&ndash; indeed, this is how _caoineadh_ is pronounced in the above clip.
Elsewhere in the recording, other verbal nouns ending in _adh_, as well as
conditional and autonomous forms, are pronounced similarly, as is the dative
singular _dhéireadh_.

The genitive singular of fire is mapped in the Linguistic Survey of Irish
Dialects, which I have been working on
[digitising](https://codeberg.org/lochsh/lasid). We can see that pronunciation
with a labial fricative at the end was not unique to the above speaker, and was
recorded in a few parts of Donegal:

<figure>
<img src="../images/teineadh-labial-fricatives.png">
</figure>

A few of these points also record a pronunciation with <span
class=ipa>/u/</span> at the end of the word, suggesting a sound-change in
progress.

In this article, I will explore the history of the pronunciation of broad «dh»,
particularly word-finally, and look at patterns of pronunciation across the
LASID and The Survey of the Gaelic Dialects of Scotland (SGDS). These records
show labial fricative pronunciations in Mid-Argyll (in conditional verb forms,
verbal nouns, and many nouns in the nominative singular), Cowal (in
conditional verb forms), Sutherland (in conditional and imperative
verb forms), parts of Munster and Connacht (in autonomous verb
forms), and an inconsistent smattering in Ulster[^data].

Additionally, there are places where words ending in «adh» generally have a
pronunciation distinct from words ending in «a», yet
which have a subset of words ending in «adh» which are pronounced as if ending
in «a». Compare Ulster «madadh» (dog) <span class=ipa>/madu:/</span> with
«biadh» (food) <span class=ipa>/biə/</span>. I will also explore common
features in the two subsets of words, in the hopes of identifying an underlying
pattern.

## A brief history of Gaelic lenited dentals

It is generally accepted that when lenition developed in Old Irish, the dental
plosives <span class=ipa>/d/</span> and <span class=ipa>/t/</span> were lenited
to dental fricatives <span class=ipa>/θ/</span> and <span class=ipa>/ð/</span>
respectively. Depending on what variety of English you speak, you might not
routinely make these sounds, but many English speakers would make these in the
words "<b>th</b>ree" and "mo<b>th</b>er" respectively.

I don't have the education to know exactly what body of evidence is behind
this reconstruction of the sounds, but we can consider that lenition resulting
in a fricative sound in the same place of articulation as the fortis sound fits
with many other fortis/lenis pairs: <span class=ipa>/b/</span> (labial plosive)
for <span class=ipa>/v/</span> (labial
fricative), <span class=ipa>/k/</span> (velar plosive) for <span
class=ipa>/x/</span> (velar fricative). We can also consider
that Latin had no dental fricative sounds, and the digraph «th» was used to
transcribe <span class=ipa>/θ/</span> from Greek and Anglo Saxon in the Latin
script. It certainly would fit for similar practice to be used in Ireland when
the Latin script was brought there.

These dental fricatives survived in the Manx recorded in the LASID[^lewis]:

| Irish orth. | Manx orth. | Transcription | English |
|-------------|------------|---------------|---------|
| madadh      | moddey     | <span class=ipa>mɑδə</span> | dog |
| éadrom      | eddrym     | <span class=ipa>ɛδəm</span> | light (adj.) |
| éadan       | eddin      | <span class=ipa>eδən</span> | face |

This non-grammatical intervocalic lenition was also a feature of Old Irish.

In O'Rahilly (1932), the evolution of these dental fricatives is
described[^rahilly]:

> As far back as the thirteenth century <i>dh</i> lost its dental character,
> and became everywhere a guttural spirant [velar fricative] (<i>gh</i>) or,
> rarely, a labial spirant [fricative] (<i>v</i>). The guttural spirant sound
> has been preserved in every dialect initially […] but in other positions it
> has, as a rule, long since disappeared from speech […]

An evolution from dental to labial fricative is familiar from so-called
"th-fronting" in English, stereotypically a feature of working class London
speech, e.g. in "muvva" for "mother", or "norf" for "north". O'Rahilly's
assertion that this change was rare seems somewhat unfounded, based on data he
himself presents and on data in the LASID and SGDS.

O'Rahilly links the evolution of broad lenited <i>b</i> and <i>m</i> from /v/
to /w/ in Ulster with the evolution of word-final «adh» from /v/ to /u/. He
also notes some of the pronunciation differentiations we shall later see in the
LASID data between homographic verb forms, where despite all being spelt the
same, the ending of autonomous, conditional, imperative and verbal noun forms
have distinct pronunciations.

[^bilabial]: It doesn't sound like it has enough friction to be labio-dental to
me, but it is quite hard to tell the difference on the noisy recording. Manus,
the speaker, has a few other speech features that Quiggin found only in the
older speakers in his 1906 study, so while Quiggin found a clear young-old
divide on labio-dental and bilabial fricatives, it's certainly not unlikely
that Manus would have had bilabial realisations. I would like to write on this
distinction in future, but it is not relevant to this investigation.

[^data]: The SGDS, as far as I could find, does not include any
autonomous/impersonal verb forms. The LASID, as far as I could find, does not
include any third-person imperative verb forms. I believe a velar fricative is
the pronunciation found in third-person imperative verb forms in parts of
Connacht and Munster. I do not know about pronunciation of autonomous forms in
the relevant areas of Scotland. No labial fricative pronunciations are
recorded in Dorian (1978) or Holmer (1938).

[^lewis]: Both voiced and unvoiced dental fricatives are also recorded on Lewis
in both the LASID and SGDS, but as innovative realisations of slender r. These
sounds are also recorded as realisations of slender r in Teelin in Wagner
(1979), particularly among speakers with dental disease (p. 30).

[^rahilly]: In general I find it a little frustrating that O'Rahilly doesn't
provide more clear citations for a lot of his claims. I don't doubt he was
drawing from deep personal knowledge, but when he simply mentions the existence
of a manuscript without saying what it is, it is frustrating. The main purpose
of this footnote is actually not to complain, but to suggest that the lack of
dental fricatives in a lot of Hibero-Englishes could perhaps be used to date
the loss of them in Irish also, but this is not something O'Rahilly mentions.
