I recently heard two quite offensive things while subjecting myself to watching
YouTube. A Canadian described adding some citrus juice to a recipe as "adding a
dollop" (how can something runny form a blob?), and an American described
the risk of adding too much gelatin to jelly as making it "stodgy" (while
something stodgy can have a thick texture, a thick texture alone does
not make something stodgy).

Now that I've got my unpleasant prescriptivist outburst out of the way, I want
to contemplate something where I truly desire only to be descriptive: food
taxonomies. Here I can confidently claim to be pure of descriptive heart: when
people ask "are hot dogs a sandwich?", I see it entirely as a question of
personal linguistic taxonomy, not a question of who is right and who is wrong.
To turn it into that seems extremely tiresome. <small>And yet I will stand by my
complaints about "dollop" and "stodgy".</small>

The below food categories fit at least one of these criteria:
my own taxonomy has confused others; others' taxonomy
has been different from my own; I know of example foods that test the
classification boundaries.

Let us begin, and discover how my brain unconsciously learnt to classify these
foods.

## Sausages

When I describe the Scottish delicacy of square sausage to English people, they
are often perplexed. "How can it be a sausage if it is not in a casing?" seems
to be their implicit complaint. When they find out that that square slice is
not uncommonly made with only beef, not pork, they lose their minds: "how is it
not a burger?!"

So, what makes something sausage to me? The inclusion of fillers such as rusk
is an important factor. I don't think you could form 100% mince into a
rectangular mould, as square sausage is formed before being sliced. If you were
able to make the correct form factor out of meat alone, would it be sausage? I
think at this point it would be a burger to me.

This brings me onto a controversial sausage opinion. In the UK, for a product
to be sold as a "pork sausage" it must contain a minimum of 42% meat. I can
vouch for this being the optimum. How do I know? Because I have bought and
tasted the lower percentage sausages, often sold as "bangers" or other
supplementary words, and found them inferior. Anything up to 60% meat remains
acceptable to me. Beyond that my enjoyment beings to drop. When I try a 90%+
meat sausage I am grossly offended. I don't like when link sausages require
chewing on bits of meat. I want to all but inhale them.

Given this opinion, do I consider ~98% meat link sausages to be sausages?
Well, I suppose I do, reluctantly[^sausage]. Clearly the form factor of a link
sausage is sufficient, though not necessary, for me to bequeath sausage status.
On the opposite end of the meat spectrum, I am happy for vegetarian sausages to
be called such. Could I accept a vegetarian square sausage? This tests my
boundaries of what I consider preposterous. I think it would require the
vegetarian square sausauge to have a suitable texture, such as if it was made
with soy protein or similar. However, I would not require the same of a
vegetarian link sausage.

Absent from the above discussion has been American uses of the word "sausage".
They clearly have a more open mind than many English people in this respect,
given they make "sausage patties" which appear to be seasoned mince
combined with delicious, delicious fillers that are then formed into
patties and fried. Would I consider this a sausage? I would. But if someone
promised me sausages and they presented me with this I would not be pleased.
Apart from desiring 42%-60% meat in my sausages, I also desire them to be
completely herb free, and americans seem very fond of adding sage and fennel
seeds to theirs. This does not affect their sausage status &mdash; in fact,
along with the inclusion of fillers like rusk, I believe salt is necessary for
something to be a sausage. Additional flavourings are optional.

With all this information, I believe we can make a list of criteria for what I
personally feel comfortable calling a sausage.

|             | Contains meat | Contains filler | Has link form factor | Has vegetal texture | Is sausage to me |
| ----------- | ----------- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Square sausage | ✔ | ✔ | ✕ | ✕ | ✔ |
| Link sausages | ✔ | ✔ | ✔ | ✕ | ✔ |
| Link sausages with 95%+ meat content | ✔ | ✕ | ✔ | ✕ | ✔ |
| Square sausage if it had no filler | ✔ | ✕ | ✕ | ✕ | ✕ |
| Vegetarian link sausages | ✕ | N/A | ✔ | ✕ | ✔ |
| More vegetabley vegetarian link sausages | ✕ | N/A | ✔ | ✔ | ✔ |
| Vegetarian square sausage | ✕ | N/A | ✕ | ✕ | ✔ |
| More vegetabley vegetarian square sausage | ✕ | N/A | ✕ | ✔ | ✕ |
| American "sausage patties" | ✔ | ✔ | ✕ | ✕ | ✔ |

To me, for something to be a sausage, then:

* if it's 100% meat, it must be in link form factor
* if it's in a slice or patty form factor, it must <b>not</b> be 100% meat
* if it contains no meat, it must satisfy at least one of: link form factor;
  non-vegetal texture.

Do not ask me about "skinless sausages": I do not understand what they are.

[^sausage]: Is not the point of a sausage to use up random bits of meat and pad
them out with delicious, delicious filler??? If you want 100% meat maybe you
should eat a steak and leave sausages alone!
